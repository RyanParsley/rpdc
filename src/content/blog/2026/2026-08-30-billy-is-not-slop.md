Some woodworkers really dislike Ikea furniture. You assemble it, but you don't
really make it. If you build 1,000 Billy bookcases, you've not really developed
any transferable skills to make other things. You may get a little faster at
understanding the wordless manuals. You may even become less likely to
misinterpret an ambiguous illustration. But you don't develop many skills that
translate beyond assembling Ikea furniture. There's no expertise to unlock. I've
seen images online where people cut into the odd table or shelf to reveal it's
not even particle board, there's paper products supplying nominal structure to
the most disappointing Kinder Egg ever.

I like Ikea for some things. I've never had need to cut into any of mine. I
understand that they aren't built of solid wood and most attempts to repair them
are doomed to fail. They do solve the problem that I purchased them for. The
plastic totes are as adequate as any other plastic tote. Billy is as sturdy as
it needs to be to hold my books. I would much rather a hardwood map storage
cabinet than an Alex, but I did not care to spend the time to build one nor
money to buy the premium option.

We have a broken Alex in one room of my house. The plastic runners don't seem to
have a mechanism for removing the tray all the way to fix it. The cardboard
bottom blew out. The underlying structure resists mending. It's a shame. I hate
that when one out of six drawers is in disrepair, throwing the whole thing out
or dealing with the jank are the only practical choices.

The thing that makes Ikea desirable (other than the price) is the design. The
designers of Ikea furniture are playing a different game than fine woodworkers.
It's often a dance of how premium can a product feel while being made from the
cheapest of raw materials. They are masters of this particular skill. Maybe the
minimal modern aesthetic isn't your vibe, but most things at an ikea are
objectively well designed.

Cutting into an Ikea shelf to reveal a shocking lack of substance makes me think
of LLM generated code. Perhaps it's hubris, but I do feel like I can architect a
codebase that is robust in spite of having generated code in it. If my tests are
good, my static analysis is pedantic and my boundaries are well defined... seems
like I can make that work. It seems plausible enough that if one is disciplined
enough to not completely "embrace the exponential" or whatever... some amount of
lines of code don't matter all that much in an artisanal way so long as the
function returns the expected value when provided a particular input.

There's a major departure between the wispy innards of Lack and slop. Slop is
very costly. Arguably, inference is less costly than human development time.
But, it also consumes developer time. So it will always be a game of diminishing
returns and tremendous opportunity cost. While the effect of slop is akin to
that of chipboard, GPUs are made of precious materials and usage is priced
accordingly. I don't know that we can learn from Ikea when it comes to agentic
software development. Part of me thinks it's possible to architect an app with
dialed-in static analysis that can hide or mitigate the slop within. But
another, maybe more rational part, resists the premise that a fundamentally
inefficient tool can be leveraged to improve productivity.
